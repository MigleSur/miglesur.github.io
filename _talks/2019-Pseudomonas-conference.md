Comparative genomics has shown that distantly related lineages of the same bacterial species differ in gene content, yet the microevolutionary dynamics of gene loss and acquisition within lineages over time remain poorly understood. We analyzed whole genome sequences from 45 *Pseudomonas aeruginosa* lineages evolving in the lungs of cystic fibrosis (CF) patients to identify genes lost or gained during the early years of infection. On average, each lineage exhibited changes in 88 genes (range: 0–473). Gene loss occurred more frequently than gene acquisition, and prophage-associated genes showed greater variability than core bacterial genes. We observed convergent patterns of gene loss and gain across independent lineages, indicating selection for specific genomic changes in the host environment. Many of these recurrently affected genes were linked to virulence, a key factor in host adaptation. Comparisons across lineages further revealed that genes variable within lineages typically belonged to accessory genomic regions rather than the shared core genome. Together, these results clarify the rate and selective forces shaping gene loss and acquisition during long-term bacterial evolution in a human host and highlight their role in lineage diversification and adaptation.
